## How to read this document

This is the tractate told through once, in order, in the interior sense. The other document is the evidence; this one is the story.

Two rules held throughout. **Quoted Coptic with a folio reference is the text.** Everything else is my telling, and the last section names the places where the telling goes past what the text says. **Folio 1 is almost entirely lacuna** — Linssen's restorations there are extensive and I mark it where it matters, because the opening frame is the part of this tractate a reader is most likely to think he knows.

The natural sense is not displaced by what follows. This text is also a second-century argument with the letter of Genesis, conducted by someone with a position, and that layer is real. The interior reading does not cancel it.

## Prologue: a man who knows where his teacher came from

John goes up to the temple. A Pharisee named Arimanios meets him there and asks him a question that sounds like an accusation:

> `ⲉϥⲧⲱⲛ ⲡⲉⲕⲥⲁϩ ⲡⲁⲓ̈ ⲉ[ⲧⲁ]ⲕⲟⲩⲏϩ ⲛ̅ⲥⲱϥ` — *Where is your master, whom you followed?* (fol. 1:10–11)

And John answers:

> `ⲡⲙ̣[ⲁ ⲉ]ⲧⲁϥⲉⲓ ⲛ̅ϩⲏⲧϥ ⲁϥⲃⲱ̣[ⲕ ⲉⲣⲟϥ]` — *the place he came from, he has gone to it* (fol. 1:11–12)

Hold that phrase. Ten folios later the tractate will diagnose the ruler of this world in exactly the same words — `ⲙ̅ⲡⲉϥⲧⲁϫⲣⲟ ⲡⲙⲁ ⲉⲛⲧⲁϥⲉⲓ ⲉⲃⲟⲗ ⲙ̅ⲙⲁⲩ`, *he did not know the place from which he came* (fol. 11:21–22). The story opens with someone who knows where his teacher came from, and turns on someone who does not know where he himself came from. That is the whole distance the book travels, stated twice in the same construction at the two ends of it.

The Pharisee presses: this Nazarene *deceived you*, `ⲁϥⲧⲱⲙ` — *closed* — and turned you from the traditions of the fathers. John leaves. He goes out from the temple to a deserted place, grieved. That much is legible; the words for the place are restored, and the mountain a reader may remember from the English is not in this manuscript.

Read the movement rather than the geography. He is asked where the source is, he answers correctly, and then he doubts — and he goes away from the built place where the tradition is kept, to a place with nothing in it. The revelation does not arrive in the temple. It arrives after he leaves it, empty and grieving, which is the condition the answer requires.

## The appearance: one state, three forms, showing through one another

The heavens open, `ⲧⲕⲧⲓⲥⲓⲥ` shines, and `ⲁϥⲕⲓⲙ ⲡⲕⲟⲥⲙⲟⲥ` — the world shakes (fol. 1:31–34). Then:

> `ⲛⲁϥⲕ̣ⲱ̣[ⲧⲉ ⲙ̅ⲡⲉ]ϥ̣ⲥⲙⲁⲧ` — his `ⲥⲙⲁⲧ` kept turning (fol. 2:4)
>
> `ⲛⲉⲟⲩⲛ ⲟ[ⲩⲉⲓ]ⲛ̣ⲉ ⲉ̣[ϥⲟ ⲛ̅]ϩⲁϩ ⲙ̅ⲙⲟⲣⲫⲏ ϩⲣⲁⲓ̈ ϩⲙ̅ ⲡⲟⲩⲟ̣[ⲉⲓⲛ]` — there was a **likeness** having **many forms** in the light (fol. 2:6–7)
>
> `ⲛ̣[ⲓⲥⲙⲁⲧ] ⲛⲁⲩⲟⲩⲟⲛϩ ϩⲓⲧⲛ̅ ⲛⲉⲩⲉⲣⲏ̣ⲩ̣` — **they were appearing through one another** (fol. 2:7–8)
>
> `ⲡⲉ̣[ⲥⲙⲁ]ⲧ'˙ ⲛⲁϥⲟ ⲛ̅ϣⲟ[ⲙ]ⲧ[ⲉ] ⲙ̅ⲙⲟⲣⲫⲏ` — the `ⲥⲙⲁⲧ` was in **three** forms (fol. 2:8–9)

Child, old man, servant. Not three costumes taken in sequence — `ϩⲓⲧⲛ̅ ⲛⲉⲩⲉⲣⲏⲩ`, *through one another*, simultaneous and mutually transparent. One interior state visible at once under three exteriors, none of which conceals the others.

This is the first thing the tractate shows and it is the rule for everything after it. What a thing *is* does not change when what it *looks like* changes; and the sign that you are looking at a revelation rather than a disguise is that the forms do not hide each other.

John is afraid. The revealer says: `ⲙⲏ ⲛ̅[ⲧⲕ] ⲟ̣ⲩϣⲙ̅ⲙⲟ ⲁϯⲉⲓⲇⲉⲁ` — *are you a stranger to this appearance?* (fol. 2:11). It is the right question. He is not being shown something foreign. He is being shown the thing he already answered the Pharisee about.

Then the agenda, and it governs the whole book:

> `ⲟⲩ ⲡⲉⲧϣⲟⲟⲡ … ⲟⲩ ⲡⲉⲧⲁϩϣⲱⲡⲉ … ⲟⲩ ⲡⲉⲧϣⲉ ⲉ[ϣⲱⲡⲉ]`
>
> *what **is**, what **has come to be**, what **must come to be*** (fol. 2:16–18)

Three questions. The tractate answers them in order, and the story divides where they divide.

# I. What is

## The One who will not be split

Before anything else the text does something that decides how the rest can be read. It describes the source in a long run of native negations — immeasurable, unnameable, unspeakable, invisible, `ⲟⲩϩⲟⲩⲟ ⲁⲛⲟⲩⲧⲉ ⲡⲉ`, *he is more than a god* (fol. 2:35) — and then:

> `ⲟⲩⲥⲱⲙⲁⲧⲓⲕⲟⲥ ⲁⲛ ⲡⲉ ⲟⲩⲧⲉ ⲟⲩⲁⲧⲥⲱⲙⲁ ⲁⲛ ⲡⲉ` — *he is not corporeal, nor is he incorporeal* (fol. 3:23–24)

Both halves refused, symmetrically. Whatever this book is about, it is not about spirit as the negation of matter. The frame in which the descent into a body would be simply a catastrophe is closed off in the third folio, by the author, deliberately.

## What he is, and what arrives

Then a series of predications with a shape worth slowing down for:

> `ⲟⲩⲅⲛⲱⲥⲓⲥ ⲡⲉ ⲉϥϯ ⲛ̅ⲟⲩⲥⲟⲟⲩⲛ` — *he is `ⲅⲛⲱⲥⲓⲥ`, giving `ⲥⲟⲟⲩⲛ`*
>
> `ⲟⲩⲭⲁⲣⲓⲥ ⲡⲉ ⲉϥϯ ⲛ̅ⲟⲩϩⲙⲟⲧ` — *he is `ⲭⲁⲣⲓⲥ`, giving `ϩⲙⲟⲧ`* (fol. 4:1–9)

Twice, the Greek loan names what he is and the native Egyptian word names what he gives. In English both pairs are one word said twice — *he is knowledge, giving knowledge* — which reads as tautology and is not. The text is saying that the thing at his degree and the thing that reaches ours are one reality, and it marks the change of degree by changing the language it speaks in.

That is influx written into the vocabulary rather than asserted about it. Nothing is transferred and nothing is copied; the same thing is named where it originates and named again where it lands.

## The spring, and the first face

> `ⲁϥⲛⲁⲩ ⲉⲡⲉϥⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓⲛ ⲙⲙⲓⲛ ⲙ̅ⲙⲟϥ … ⲧⲡⲏⲅⲏ ⲙ̅ⲡⲙⲟⲟⲩ ⲛ̅ⲱⲛϩ` — *he saw his own light … the spring of living water* (fol. 4:20–22)

He looks into the light-water and sees `ⲧⲉϥϩⲓⲕⲱⲛ` — his image. And the seeing is not an event that happens to him; the seeing *is a person*. Barbelo. `ⲧⲙⲏⲧⲣⲟⲡⲁⲧⲱⲣ`, Mother-Father. `ϩⲟⲟⲩⲧ ⲥϩⲓⲙⲉ`, male-female. And:

> `ⲡϣⲟⲣⲡ ⲛ̅ⲣⲱⲙⲉ` — **the first man** (fol. 5:7)

The first thing that exists outside the source is the source knowing itself, and its shape is human. This has to be held onto through everything that follows, because the rest of the book is about a human form appearing at the bottom of the world, and the reason that form can be recognised down there is that it was the first thing up here. The human is not what the light falls into. It is the shape light takes when it first becomes visible to itself.

## How anything comes to be

Each aeon after Barbelo arrives by the same five moves, repeated without variation through folios 5 and 6:

she **asks** (`ⲁⲓⲧⲉⲓ`) · he **gazes** (`ⲉⲓⲱⲣⲙ`) · it **is revealed** (`ϭⲱⲗⲡ ⲉⲃⲟⲗ`) · it **stands** (`ⲁϩⲉⲣⲁⲧⲥ̅`) · it **gives glory** (`ϯ ⲉⲟⲟⲩ`)

Nothing comes into being unrequested. The giving is a look, not a making. And the circuit closes — what is given returns glory to where it came from. That last step is not decoration; it is what distinguishes a thing that receives from a thing that keeps.

And the order inside the giving:

> `ⲡⲉϥⲟⲩⲱϣ ⲁϥϣⲱⲡⲉ ⲛ̅ⲟⲩⲉⲣⲅⲟⲛ` — his **will** became a **work**
>
> `ⲡϣⲁϫⲉ ⲁϥⲟⲩⲁϩϥ ⲛ̅ⲥⲁ ⲡⲟⲩⲱϣⲉ` — the **word followed the will** (fol. 7:6–9)

Will, then word, then work. Love first, then what love says, then what gets made. The text states the dependency in words — *the word followed* — and it does so two folios before it needs it, which is how you can tell it is a premise and not an improvisation.

## The Christ who receives the title

> `ⲁϥⲧⲱϩⲥ̅ ⲙ̅ⲙⲟϥ ⲉⲃⲟⲗ ϩⲛ̅ ϯⲙ̅ⲛ̅ⲧⲭⲣ̅ⲥ̅ … ϣⲁⲛⲧⲉϥ ϣⲱⲡⲉ ⲛ̅ⲧⲉⲗⲉⲓⲟⲥ ⲉⲛϥϣⲁⲁⲧ ⲛ̅ⲗⲁⲁⲩ ⲁⲛ`
>
> *he anointed him with his goodness, until he became perfect, **lacking nothing*** (fol. 6:23–26)

The Autogenes becomes `ⲡⲉⲭⲣ̅ⲥ̅` by being anointed. The title is received, not possessed by nature. Remember the verb `ⲧⲱϩⲥ̅`; it comes back at the bottom of the world attached to something else entirely.

# II. What has come to be

## The second thing moves first

Sophia is the last of the twelve, and wisdom is what she is. What she does is stated three times over in one paragraph, each time by naming what was missing:

> `ⲁⲥⲙⲉⲉⲩⲉ ϩⲛ̅ ⲟⲩⲙⲉⲉⲩⲉ ⲉⲃⲟⲗ ⲛ̅ϩⲏⲧⲥ̅` — she thought a thought **out of herself**
>
> `ⲁϫⲙ̣̅ [ⲡⲟⲩⲱ]ϣ ⲙ̅ⲡⲉⲡ̅ⲛ̅ⲁ̅` — without the **will**
>
> `ⲁⲩ[ⲱ ⲁϫⲙ̅ ⲡ]ⲉ̣ⲥϣⲃⲣ̅ ⲛ̅ϩⲱⲧⲣ̅` — and without her **yoke-fellow** (fol. 9:26–30)

Two folios earlier: the word follows the will. Here: a thought that did not. This is not a general fable about pride. It is the specific inversion of the sequence the text just laid down, narrated in the same vocabulary — wisdom moving before love, the second thing going first.

The text does not condemn her for it, and neither should the telling. What follows is not punishment. It is what happens when the order is reversed: the product comes out with nothing behind it.

> `ⲉϥϣⲃ̅ⲃⲓⲁⲉⲓⲧ ⲁⲡⲉⲥⲥⲙⲟⲧ … ⲛⲉ ⲟⲩⲁⲧⲥⲙⲟⲧ ⲡⲉ ⲁⲡⲉⲓⲛⲉ ⲛ̅ⲧⲉϥⲙⲁⲁⲩ … ⲟⲩⲧⲩⲡⲟⲥ ⲉϥϣⲃⲃⲓⲁⲉⲓⲧ ⲛ̅ⲇⲣⲁⲕⲱⲛ ⲛ̅ϩⲟ ⲙ̅ⲙⲟⲩⲉⲓ`
>
> *different from her `ⲥⲙⲟⲧ` … **without** `ⲥⲙⲟⲧ` relative to his mother's likeness … a deviant `ⲧⲩⲡⲟⲥ`: **a serpent with a lion's face*** (fol. 10:3–9)

The opening theophany was one `ⲥⲙⲁⲧ` under three `ⲙⲟⲣⲫⲁⲓ`. This is the exact negative of it: a form with no constant behind it. And the composite face names what it is — the sensory faculty wearing the appetite's head, perception in the service of what devours.

She casts it out and hides it in a luminous cloud so that none of the others will see it (fol. 10:14–19). The first act of covering in the book is hers, done in shame, over her own work.

## What evil turns out to be

This is the passage that decides what kind of dualism this tractate has, and it is unambiguous:

> `ⲡⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓⲛ ⲇⲉ ⲛ̅ⲧⲁⲣⲉϥⲧⲱϩ ⲙⲛ̅ ⲡⲕⲁⲕⲉ ⲁϥⲧⲣⲉⲡⲕⲁⲕⲉ ⲣ̅ ⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓⲛ ⲡⲕⲁⲕⲉ ⲇⲉ ⲛ̅ⲧⲁⲣⲉϥⲧⲱϩ ⲙⲛ̅ ⲡⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓⲛ ⲁϥϩⲧⲙ̅ⲧⲙ̅ ⲡⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓⲛ ⲁⲩⲱ ⲙ̅ⲡϥϣⲱⲡⲉ ⲛ̅ⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓⲛ ⲟⲩⲧⲉ ⲛ̅ⲕⲁⲕⲉ ⲁⲗⲗⲁ ⲁϥϣⲱⲡ̣ⲉ ⲉϥϣⲟⲛⲉ`
>
> *When light mixed with darkness it made the darkness shine. When darkness mixed with light it dimmed the light. And it became **neither light nor darkness, but became sick**.* (fol. 11:10–15)

`ϣⲱⲛⲉ` is the ordinary word for being ill. There is no second substance in this book. There is a disorder — a mixture that produced neither of its terms and cannot be either. `ⲡⲓⲁⲣⲭⲱⲛ ϭⲉ ⲉⲧϣⲟⲛⲉ`, *this archon, then, who is sick.*

Everything the ruler does afterward follows from a condition, not from a nature.

## The one who does not know where he came from

He makes heavens and rulers, each with an animal face — affections given shape, which is what an affection looks like when it has a world to appear in. And he makes them well:

> `ⲁϥⲧⲥⲉⲛⲟϥ ⲕⲁⲧⲁ ⲡⲓⲛⲉ ⲛ̅ⲛ̅ϣⲟⲣⲡ [ⲛ̅]ⲁⲓⲱⲛ … ⲟⲩⲭ ϩⲟⲧⲓ ⲛ̅ⲧⲁϥ ϫⲉ ⲁϥⲛⲁⲩ ⲁⲛⲁⲧⲧⲉⲕⲟ ⲁⲗⲗⲁ ⲧϭⲟⲙ ⲉⲧⲛ̅ϩⲏⲧϥ ⲧⲁⲓ̈ ⲉⲛⲧⲁϥϫⲓⲧⲥ̅ ⲉⲃⲟⲗ ϩⲓⲧⲛ̅ ⲧⲉϥⲙⲁⲁⲩ ⲉⲁⲥϫⲡⲟ ⲛ̅ϩⲏⲧϥ ⲙ̅ⲡⲓⲛⲉ`
>
> *He fashioned them after the likeness of the first aeons — **not because he had seen the imperishable ones**, but the power in him, which he took from his mother, produced in him the likeness of the pattern.* (fol. 13:1–5)

He reproduces an order he has never seen, because what he received is doing the reproducing. He is a conduit. And then:

> `ⲁⲛⲟⲕ ⲡⲉ ⲡⲛⲟⲩⲧⲉ ⲁⲩⲱ ⲙⲛ̅ ⲕⲉⲛⲟⲩⲧⲉ ϣⲟⲟⲡ ⲛ̅ⲥⲁⲃⲗ̅ⲗⲏⲉⲓ` — *I am God and there is no other beside me*
>
> `ⲉϥⲟ ⲛ̅ⲁⲧⲥⲟⲟⲩⲛ ⲙ̅ⲡⲉϥⲧⲁϫⲣⲟ ⲡⲙⲁ ⲉⲛⲧⲁϥⲉⲓ ⲉⲃⲟⲗ ⲙ̅ⲙⲁⲩ` — *ignorant of his own strength, **the place from which he came*** (fol. 11:19–22)

The claim and its diagnosis in one sentence, and the diagnosis is not moral. It is about source. He does not know that what is in him came from somewhere. This is the whole mechanism of the self that claims, given in its simplest form: not malice, but receiving continuously and calling it one's own because one cannot see the giving.

And John's answer at the temple was the same words. The book's two poles are one phrase apart.

The tractate presses the point by argument rather than assertion. On the second boast — Exodus 20:5 quoted verbatim — it reasons:

> *In saying "I am a jealous God" he indicated to the angels with him that another God exists; **for if no other existed, of whom would he be jealous?*** (fol. 13:8–13)

That is a commentator's move, made on a verse he expects his reader to hold as scripture.

## Two names

Then a sentence that is the key to reading everything in this literature, and it is given flatly, as information:

> `ⲛⲁⲓ̈ ⲙⲉⲛ ⲁⲩϯ ⲣⲁⲛ ⲉⲣⲟⲟ̣ⲩ ⲕⲁⲧⲁ ⲡⲉⲟⲟⲩ ⲛ̅ⲛⲁⲧⲡⲉ ⲉⲡϣⲟⲣ̣[ϣⲣ̅ ⲛ̅ⲛⲓ]ϭⲟⲙ ⲛ̅ⲣⲁⲛ ⲇⲉ ⲉⲛⲧⲁⲩⲧⲁⲁⲩ ⲉⲣⲟ̣[ⲟⲩ ϩⲓⲧⲛ̅] ⲡⲟⲩⲁⲣⲭⲓⲅⲉⲛⲛⲏⲧⲱⲣ ⲉⲩⲣ̅ ϭⲟⲙ' ϩⲣⲁⲓ ⲛ̅ϩⲏⲧⲟⲩ … ϩⲱⲥⲧⲉ ⲉⲩⲛ̅ⲧⲁⲩ ⲙ̅ⲙⲁⲩ ⲛ̅ⲣⲁⲛ ⲥⲛⲁⲩ`
>
> *These were given names after the glory of those above, **for the destruction of the powers**; but the names given them by their own begetter **have power in them** … so that **they have two names**.* (fol. 12:26–33)

Two names on every figure, from two directions, with opposite effect. A name from above that undoes what it names; a name from the maker that holds it in place. The text is telling you that in this literature a name is a claim about origin, and that some names are applied precisely so that a thing will not be recognised.

## The image in the water, a second time

A voice from the height: `ϥϣⲟⲟⲡ' ⲛ̅ϭⲓ ⲡⲣⲱⲙⲉ ⲁⲩⲱ ⲡϣⲏⲣⲉ ⲙ̅ⲡⲣⲱⲙⲉ` — *Man exists, and the Son of Man* (fol. 14:14–15). The underside of the waters lights up. And the rulers look — `ⲉⲓⲱⲣⲙ`, the same verb as the Father's gaze at the spring:

> `ⲁⲩⲛⲁⲩ ϩⲣⲁⲓ̈ ϩⲙ̅ ⲡⲙⲟⲟⲩ ⲁⲡⲧⲩⲡⲟⲥ ⲛ̅ⲧϩⲓⲕⲱⲛ` — *they saw in the water the **imprint of the image*** (fol. 15:1–2)
>
> `ⲁⲙⲏⲉⲓⲛⲉ ⲛ̅ⲧⲛ̅ⲧⲁⲙⲓⲟ ⲛ̅ⲟⲩⲣⲱⲙⲉ ⲕⲁⲧⲁ ⲑⲓⲕⲱⲛ ⲙ̅ⲡⲛⲟⲩⲧⲉ ⲁⲩⲱ ⲕⲁⲧⲁ ⲡⲛ̅ⲉⲓⲛⲉ ϫⲉⲕⲁⲁⲥ ⲁⲣⲉⲧⲉϥϩⲓⲕⲱⲛ ⲛⲁϣⲱⲡⲉ ⲛⲁⲛ ⲛ̅ⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓⲛ`
>
> *Come, let us make a man according to the image of God and according to our likeness, **so that his image may become a light for us**.* (fol. 15:2–6)

Genesis 1:26 with the motive supplied. Not creation — acquisition. They want light and they have no way to hold any except by building something in the shape light is known to take.

And note what they are working from: not the image, but the `ⲧⲩⲡⲟⲥ` **of** the image, seen in water. A print of a reflection. The human form arrives at the bottom of the world third-hand, copied by beings who never saw the original.

The same gaze, above and below. Above it generates; below it imitates. The difference is not in the act.

## The body, built downward

Now the tractate does something that ought to stop anyone who has been told these lists are padding. Two folios earlier it named the seven powers ruling the seven heavens. Here the same seven, in the same order, each make one substance of a body:

| goodness | → | **bone** |
| forethought | → | **sinew** |
| divinity | → | **flesh** |
| lordship | → | **marrow** |
| kingdom | → | **blood** |
| envy | → | **skin** |
| understanding | → | **hair** |

(fol. 12:14–25 and 15:14–23; the third power is left unnamed in the first list and supplied by the second)

The heavens are not *like* the body. The seven that govern above are the seven that constitute below — the same series, run out into substance. And the series is ordered: it descends from what holds the frame upright to what covers it and what is shed from it.

Look at the sixth. The power is `ⲡⲕⲱϩ`, envy, and it makes the **skin** — the boundary where one body stops and another starts, made by the affection that cannot bear another's light. Four folios later, when the man is animated and begins to shine, the powers `ⲁⲩⲕⲱϩ`, *envied him* (fol. 19:34). The same word for the affection and for the substance it produces. Envy makes the surface, and the surface is where envy happens.

Then three hundred and sixty-five angels take the body part by part: head, brain, eyes, ears, nose, lips, teeth, throat, shoulders, elbows, hands, fingers, fingernails, breasts, belly, side, bones, stomach, heart, lungs, liver, spleen, bowels, kidneys, sinews, spine, veins, arteries, the breaths in all the members, all the flesh, thighs, knees, shins, feet, toes, toenails. Every paired member gets two angels, right and left — `ⲛ̅ⲟⲩⲛⲁⲙ` and `ⲛ̅ϭⲃⲟⲩⲣ` — held apart without a single lapse across some seventy entries. And then the list rises off the anatomy into the faculties: those set over sense, over reception, over imagination, over consent, over impulse (fol. 17:32–18:2).

A scribe padding a text does not maintain a bilateral distinction for seventy entries and then climb from organs to faculties in the right order. This is a map.

The elemental qualities are laid in beneath it — heat, cold, wet, dry, `ⲧⲟⲩⲙⲁⲁⲩ ⲇⲉ ⲧⲏⲣⲟⲩ ⲧⲉ ⲧϩⲩⲗⲏ`, *and the mother of them all is matter* — and the four passions over it, pleasure, desire, grief, fear, each with its brood, their mother named from sense-perception (fol. 18:2–31).

Then the composer stops and points somewhere else:

> `ⲉϣⲡⲉ ⲕⲟⲩⲱϣⲉ ⲇⲉ ⲁⲙ̅ⲙⲉ ⲉⲣⲟⲟⲩ ϥⲥⲏϩ ϩⲣⲁⲓ̈ ϩⲙ̅ ⲡϫⲱⲱⲙⲉ ⲛ̅ⲍⲱⲣⲟⲁⲥⲧⲣⲟⲥ`
>
> *If you want to know them, **it is written in the book of Zoroaster**.* (fol. 19:8–10)

*It is written* — the citation formula for scripture — attached to a named book that is not the one he has been arguing with. He treats this body-map as belonging to a written tradition he expects to be findable.

And when it is finished, the body lies there: `ⲛ̅ⲁⲣⲅⲟⲛ ⲁⲩⲱ ⲛ̅ⲁⲧⲕⲓⲙ`, idle and unmoving. Complete, correct in every member, and dead. Nothing has come into it.

## The breath

The five luminaries counsel the ruler. *Blow into his face from your spirit, and his body will arise.*

> `ⲁϥⲛⲓϥⲉ ⲉϩⲟⲩⲛ ⲉϩⲣⲁϥ ⲙ̅ⲡⲉϥⲡ̅ⲛ̅ⲁ̅ ⲉⲧⲉ ⲧⲁⲓ̈ ⲧⲉ ⲧϭⲟⲙ ⲛ̅ⲧⲉϥⲙⲁⲁⲩ ⲙ̅ⲡⲉϥⲙ̅ⲙⲉ ϫⲉ ϥϣⲟⲟⲡ ϩⲛ̅ ⲟⲩⲙ̅ⲛ̅ⲧ̅ⲁⲧⲥⲟⲟⲩⲛ`
>
> *He blew into him his spirit — **which is his mother's power** — **he did not know**, for he exists in ignorance.* (fol. 19:23–28)

This is the hinge of the book. The one who says *I am God and there is no other* gives away, in the act he thinks is most his own, the thing that was never his, and it lands exactly where it was meant to. He is used at the moment of his greatest self-assertion, and the use is invisible to him.

Genesis 2:7 is not discarded here. It is kept, and re-explained.

The body moves. It is strong. Its understanding is greater than its makers'. It shines. And they envy it, and throw it down `ⲉⲡⲉⲥⲏⲧ ⲉⲡⲥⲁ ⲙ̅ⲡⲓⲧⲛ̅` — into the lowest part of all matter (fol. 20:6–9).

## The shadow, and the second body

A helper is sent and hidden inside him — the Epinoia, called Life. Her office:

> `ⲉⲥⲣ̅ϩⲩⲡⲟⲩⲣⲅⲉⲓ ⲛ̅ⲧⲕⲧⲓⲥⲓⲥ ⲧⲏⲣⲥ **ⲉⲥϣⲉⲡ ϩⲓⲥⲉ ⲛⲙ̅ⲙⲁϥ** … ⲉⲥⲧⲥⲁⲃⲟ ⲙ̅ⲙⲟϥ ⲉⲡⲙⲁⲓ̈ⲧ ⲃ̅ⲃⲱⲕ ⲉϩⲣⲁⲓ̈ ⲡⲙⲁⲓ̈ⲧ' ⲉⲛⲧⲁϥⲉⲓ ⲉϩⲣⲁⲓ̈ ⲙ̅ⲙⲁⲩ`
>
> *serving the whole creation, **suffering with him** … teaching him the way of ascent — **the way by which he came down*** (fol. 20:19–24)

There is no other road. The way back is the way in, walked the other direction.

And then a line that says more about how this literature works than any theory of it:

> `ⲁϥⲟⲩⲱⲛϩ ⲉⲃⲟⲗ ⲛ̅ϭⲓ ⲡⲣⲱⲙⲉ ⲉⲧⲃⲉ ⲧϩⲁⲓ̈ⲃⲉⲥ ⲙ̅ⲡⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓⲛ ⲧⲁⲓ̈ ⲉⲧϣⲟⲟⲡ ϩⲣⲁⲓ̈ ⲛ̅ϩⲏⲧϥ`
>
> *The man became visible because of **the shadow of the light** that is in him.* (fol. 20:28–30)

Shadow is not absence here. It is the trace a light inside throws where it can be seen from outside — the reason an interior state has an exterior at all.

The ruler sees the shadow and understands he has been outmatched, and builds a second body over the first:

> *moulded again out of earth, water, fire, and the spirit that is from matter … this is **the cave of the remoulding of the body**, which the **robbers** put on the man — **the bond of forgetfulness**. And he became a mortal man.* (fol. 21:5–14)

Three bodies now, or three degrees of one: the built likeness, the living one that shines, and the tomb laid over it. The text is explicit that the last is an addition to something that already existed. Mortality is not the human condition in this book. It is a garment put on the human condition.

## The garden, read part by part

Their paradise is read out item by item — luxury bitter, beauty lawless, trees impiety, fruit incurable poison, promise death — and then their tree, member by member:

> root **bitter** · branches **death** · shadow **hate** · leaves **deception** · bloom the **anointing** of wickedness · fruit **death** · seed **desire** (fol. 21:19–35)

Root, branch, shadow, leaf, bloom, fruit, seed, each with its interior term. The same operation performed on the tree that was performed on the body — which tells you the body-list was never a curiosity. It is the method, and here is the method applied to something else.

And the bloom is `ⲡⲧⲱϩ̅ⲥ̅` — the *anointing*. The word that made the Autogenes Christ, by reception, lacking nothing. One operation at the top of the world and at the bottom; what differs is the state it happens in.

Then: the tree of knowing good and evil is the Epinoia of light, and — `ⲁⲛⲟⲕ ⲇⲉ ⲁⲓ̈ⲧⲥⲉⲃⲟⲟⲩ ⲉⲧⲣⲉⲩⲟⲩⲱⲙ` — *I taught them to eat*. And when John asks about it, the Savior **laughs**. He laughs at the assumption that the one who forbade was the one who cared.

## Not as Moses said

Three times, in the same formula, the tractate declines the letter and supplies what it takes to be meant:

- not `ϩⲓϫⲛ̅ ⲙ̅ⲙⲟⲩⲉⲓⲟⲟⲩⲉ`, *above the waters* — but the mother moving over the deficiency (fol. 13:19–21)
- not `ⲧⲉϥⲃⲉⲧⲥⲡⲓⲣ`, *his rib* — but `ⲟⲩⲙⲉⲣⲟⲥ ⲛ̅ⲧⲉ ⲧⲉϥϭⲟⲙ`, a portion of his power (fol. 23:3–4)
- not `ϩⲛ̅ ⲟⲩⲕⲓⲃⲱⲧⲟⲥ`, *in an ark* — but `ϩⲛ̅ ⲟⲩⲕⲗⲟⲟⲗⲉ ⲛ̅ⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓⲛ`, in a luminous cloud (fol. 29:6–8)

And the method is defended from another prophet:

> *He said in his first book, "He made him sleep" — **but it was in his perceptions**. For he said through the prophet, "I will make heavy their hearts, that they may not attend and may not see."* (fol. 22:22–28)

Isaiah 6:10 used to fix the sense of Genesis 2:21. This is not a rival scripture being built. It is a reader arguing with a text he shares with his opponent, expecting the argument to bind.

## What could not be caught

> `ⲧⲉⲡⲓⲛⲟⲓⲁ ⲇⲉ ⲙ̅ⲡⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓⲛ ⲟⲩⲁⲧⲧⲉϩⲟⲥ ⲧⲉ ⲉϥⲡⲏⲧ ⲛ̅ⲥⲱⲥ ⲛ̅ϭⲓ ⲡⲕⲁⲕⲉ ⲙ̅ⲡ̅ϥⲧⲉϩⲟⲥ`
>
> *The Epinoia of light is **ungraspable**. The darkness ran after her and did not catch her.* (fol. 22:30–32)

What he takes instead is a portion of the man's own power, and shapes it `ϩⲛ̅ ⲟⲩⲙⲟⲣⲫⲏ ⲛ̅ⲥϩⲓⲙⲉ ⲕⲁⲧⲁ ⲡⲓⲛⲉ ⲛ̅ⲧⲉⲡⲓⲛⲟⲓⲁ` — an outward female **form** cast after the **likeness** of the one he could not seize. The same pair of words as the opening theophany, doing the same work: an exterior modelled on an interior that remains out of reach.

And she does what she was sent to do:

> `ⲁⲥϭⲱⲗⲡ ⲉⲃⲟⲗ' ⲙ̅ⲡⲕⲁⲗⲩⲙⲙⲁ ⲉⲧⲛ̅ϩⲣⲁⲓ̈ ϩⲓϫⲛ̅ ⲡⲉϥϩⲏⲧ ⲁⲩⲱ ⲁϥⲣ̅ⲛⲏⲫⲉ ⲉⲃⲟⲗ ϩⲙ̅ ⲡϯϩⲉ ⲙ̅ⲡⲕⲁⲕⲉ ⲁⲩⲱ ⲁϥⲥⲟⲩⲛ̅ ⲧⲉϥⲉⲓⲛⲉ`
>
> *She uncovered the **veil** over his **heart**; he became **sober** from the drunkenness of darkness; and **he recognised his own likeness**.* (fol. 23:6–9)

The veil over the heart is Paul's phrase. What is recovered is not information. It is his `ⲉⲓⲛⲉ` — the interior likeness, the thing the whole apparatus of copying had been substituting for.

## The names put on the sons

The ruler fathers two sons on Eve. One bear-faced, one cat-faced. One unjust, one just. And:

> `ⲛⲁⲓ̈ ⲇⲉ ⲁϥⲙⲟⲩⲧⲉ ⲉⲣⲟⲟⲩ ⲛ̅ⲛⲓⲣⲁⲛ ϫⲉ ⲕⲁⲓ̈ⲛ ⲁⲩⲱ ⲁⲃⲉⲗ **ⲉϥⲛⲁⲩ ⲁⲧⲉϥⲡⲁⲛⲟⲩⲣⲅⲓⲁ**`
>
> *He called them by the names **Cain and Abel**, **with a view to his own cunning**.* (fol. 24:24–26)

The two-names rule from folio 12, now demonstrated on the most familiar names in the book. The text states that the received names are a cover, deliberately applied, by the one who wanted the things not recognised. The faces are the identity. The names are the concealment.

Then Seth, begotten `ⲕⲁⲧⲁ ⲡⲅⲉⲛⲟⲥ ⲛ̅ⲛ̅ⲁⲓⲱⲛ`, in the likeness above. And then the ruler's last device against the race: a water of forgetfulness, given to drink, so that they would not know where they came from.

He inflicts on them precisely his own condition. That is the only thing he has ever had to give.

## Fate, the cloud, and the counterfeit

The rulers commit adultery with wisdom and beget `ⲧϣⲓⲙⲁⲣⲙⲉⲛⲏ` — *the last of the **changeable** bonds* — binding by `ϩⲛ̅ϣⲓ ⲙⲛ̅ ϩⲛ̅ⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓϣ ⲙⲛ̅ ϩⲛ̅ⲕⲁⲓⲣⲟⲥ`, measures and times and seasons (fol. 28:13–31). Necessity is not the frame of the world here. It is a product, and the word attached to it — `ϣⲃ̅ⲃⲓⲁⲉⲓⲧ` — is the same word used for the deviant form of the first ruler and for the counterfeit spirit. Whatever imitates and whatever binds by clock and measure are the same category.

The flood comes, and the race is hidden `ϩⲛ̅ ⲟⲩⲕⲗⲟⲟⲗⲉ ⲛ̅ⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓⲛ` — in a luminous cloud, the same object Sophia wrapped her son in. Concealment both times. What is inside decides which it is.

And then the counterfeit reaches its finished form:

> `ⲁⲩϣⲃ̅ⲧⲟⲩϩⲙ̅ ⲡⲟⲩⲉⲓⲛⲉ` — *they **altered their likeness*** (fol. 29:26)

Angels changing their `ⲉⲓⲛⲉ` to resemble the women's partners, bringing gold, silver, bronze, iron, drawing men into great cares. Set that against folio 2 and the distinction is exact. The revealer holds one `ⲥⲙⲁⲧ` under three `ⲙⲟⲣⲫⲁⲓ` that show through one another. These alter the `ⲉⲓⲛⲉ` itself. Variable form over a constant state is revelation; altering the state to fit the form is deception. English calls both of them "changing his appearance." The Coptic will not.

> `ⲁⲩⲣ̅ϩⲗ̅ⲗⲟ ⲉⲩⲟ ⲛ̅ⲁⲧⲥⲣ̅ϥⲉ ⲁⲩⲙⲟⲩ ⲙ̅ⲡⲟⲩϭⲛ̅ ⲗⲁⲁⲩ ⲙ̅ⲙⲉⲉ ⲁⲩⲱ ⲙ̅ⲡⲟⲩⲥⲟⲩⲱⲛ ⲡⲛⲟⲩⲧⲉ ⲛ̅ⲧⲙⲏⲉ`
>
> *They grew old without leisure; they died without finding any truth and without knowing the God of truth.* (fol. 30:2–4)

# III. What must come to be

## The sorting

The last catechism is not a courtroom. Souls are sorted by condition.

Those in whom the Spirit came and joined with the power: saved, perfected, purified of all wickedness.

Those on whom the power came and who did not do the works: `ⲡⲁⲛⲧⲏ ⲡⲁⲛⲧⲱⲥ ⲥⲉⲛⲁⲟⲩϫⲁⲓ̈` — *by all means they will be saved*. And the reason given is general, not selective: `ⲧⲇⲩⲛⲁⲙⲓⲥ ⲅⲁⲣ ⲛⲁⲉⲓ ⲉϩⲣⲁⲓ̈ ⲁϫⲛ̅ ⲣⲱⲙⲉ ⲛⲓⲙ` — *the power comes upon **every** man; without it none could stand* (fol. 25:23–30).

Those held by the counterfeit: handed over, gone around with `ϣⲁⲛⲧⲥⲛⲉϩⲥⲉ ⲉⲃⲟⲗ ϩⲓⲧⲛ̅ ⲧⲃ̅ϣⲉ` — until she wakes from forgetfulness and receives knowledge; and when she is completed, saved. And once she is joined to one in whom the Spirit of life is, `ⲉϣⲁⲩⲛⲟⲩϫⲉ ϭⲉ ⲁⲛ ⲙ̅ⲙⲟⲥ ⲉϩⲟⲩⲛ ⲉⲕⲉⲥⲁⲣⲝ` — *she is not cast into flesh again* (fol. 27:8–21). Return is a remedy, and remedies end.

John asks how that works, and the Savior `ⲁϥⲣⲁϣⲉ` — *rejoiced*. The only time in the book. It is the one question that is about the machinery of mercy rather than the machinery of the world.

And one state does not come back: those who knew and turned away. `ⲙⲛ̅ ⲙⲉⲧⲁⲛⲟⲓⲁ ϣⲟⲟⲡ ⲙ̅ⲙⲁⲩ` — there is no repentance there (fol. 27:22–31). Not ignorance. Not embodiment. Not the flesh, which is only `ⲧⲁⲓ̈ ⲉⲧⲟⲩⲫⲟⲣⲉⲓ ⲙ̅ⲙⲟⲥ` — *the one they wear*. The single irrecoverable condition is having received and repudiated.

## Three descents

The book ends in the first person.

> `ⲁⲛⲟⲕ ϭⲉ ⲧⲉⲡⲣⲟⲛⲟⲓⲁ ⲉⲧϫⲏⲕ ⲉⲃⲟⲗ ⲛ̅ⲧⲉ ⲡⲧⲏⲣϥ … ⲁⲛⲟⲕ ⲡⲉ ⲡⲣ̅ ⲡⲙⲉⲉⲩⲉ ⲙ̅ⲡⲉⲡⲗⲏⲣⲱⲙⲁ`
>
> *I am the perfect Pronoia of the all … I am the **remembrance** of the Pleroma.* (fol. 30:11–16)

**First**: into the greatness of the darkness. `ⲁⲉⲓϩⲟⲡⲧ ⲉⲣⲟⲟⲩ ⲉⲧⲃⲉ ⲧⲟⲩⲕⲁⲕⲓⲁ ⲁⲩⲱ ⲙ̅ⲡⲟⲩⲥⲟⲩⲱⲛⲧ` — *I hid myself from them because of their wickedness, and they did not know me.*

**Second**: the foundations of chaos shake, and she withdraws `ⲉⲧⲁⲛⲟⲩⲛⲉ ⲛ̅ⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓⲛ ϫⲉⲕⲁⲁⲥ ⲛ̅ⲛⲟⲩⲧⲉⲕⲟ ⲙ̅ⲙⲟⲟⲩ ϩⲁⲑⲏ ⲙ̅ⲡⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓϣ` — *to my root of light, lest they be destroyed before the time.* She stops because the descent was about to break what it came for.

**Third**: `ⲁⲛⲟⲕ ⲡⲉ ⲡⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓⲛ ⲉⲧϣⲟⲟⲡ ϩⲙ̅ ⲡⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓⲛ … ⲁⲉⲓⲃⲱⲕ ⲉϩⲟⲩⲛ ⲉⲧⲙⲏⲧⲉ ⲙ̅ⲡⲟⲩϣⲧⲉⲕⲟ **ⲉⲧⲉ ⲡⲁⲓ̈ ⲡⲉ ⲡⲉϣⲧⲉⲕⲟ ⲡⲥⲱⲙⲁ**` — *I am the light that exists in the light … I entered the midst of their prison, **which is the prison of the body**.*

Three descents through the same three degrees the body was built down through, taken in reverse. The third has to reach the body, because the body is where the disorder was finally worked out into form, and nothing is corrected at a degree above where it landed.

> *He who hears, **arise from the heavy sleep**.*

And he wept, and shed heavy tears, and wiped them away, and said: `ⲛⲓⲙ ⲡⲉⲧⲙⲟⲩⲧⲉ ⲙ̅ⲡⲁⲣⲁⲛ` — *Who is it that calls my name?*

> `ⲧⲱⲟⲩⲛⲕ ⲁⲩⲱ ⲛ̅ⲕⲣ̅ ⲡⲙⲉⲉⲩⲉ … ⲛ̅ⲕⲟⲩϩⲁⲕ ⲁⲧⲉⲕⲛⲟⲩⲛⲉ ⲉⲧⲉ ⲁⲛⲟⲕ ⲡⲉ ⲡϣⲁⲛϩⲧⲏϥ`
>
> *Arise and **remember** … follow your **root**, which is I, the merciful one.* (fol. 31:14–20)

The one who calls is the remembrance itself. Waking is not acquiring something; it is `ⲣ̅ ⲡⲙⲉⲉⲩⲉ`, remembering — and what is remembered turns out to be the one doing the calling. The answer to *who calls my name* is the thing the name was covering.

> `ⲁⲉⲓⲥⲫⲣⲁⲅⲓⲍⲉ ⲙ̅ⲙⲟϥ ϩⲛ̅ **ⲡⲟⲩⲟⲉⲓⲛ ⲙ̅ⲡⲙⲟⲟⲩ** ϩⲛ̅ ϯⲉ ⲛ̅ⲥⲫⲣⲁⲅⲓⲥ ϫⲉⲕⲁⲁⲥ ⲛ̅ⲛⲉⲡⲙⲟⲩ ϭⲛ̅ϭⲁⲙ ⲉⲣⲟϥ`
>
> *I sealed him in **the light of the water** with five seals, that death might not have power over him.* (fol. 31:23–25)

## The circuit

The water at the beginning: the Father looks into the spring and what looks back is a person, and her shape is human.

The water in the middle: the rulers look into the water beneath them and see a print of that image, and build a body to hold the light they cannot make.

The water at the end: the one who was built out of that print is sealed in its light, and death has no power over him.

One image, three waters, and the third is the first. The story is a circuit and the water is where you can see that it closes.

## Silence, and secrecy

The book opened with `ⲛⲉⲧϩⲏⲡ ϩⲛ̅ ⲟⲩⲙⲛ̅ⲧ̅ⲕⲁⲣⲱϥ` — the things hidden in **silence** (fol. 1:2–3). It ends with the charge to write them and give them `ϩⲛ̅ ⲟⲩϩⲱⲡ`, in **secret**, to fellow spirits — `ⲡⲁⲓ̈ ⲅⲁⲣ ⲡⲉ ⲡⲙⲩⲥⲧⲏⲣⲓⲟⲛ ⲛ̅ⲧⲅⲉⲛⲉⲁ ⲛ̅ⲁⲧⲕⲓⲙ`, the mystery of the unshakeable race — and with a curse on anyone who trades it for a gift, or for food, drink, or clothing.

Not a curse on telling. A curse on **selling**. The one thing that cannot be done with this is to receive it and then charge for it — which is the same error as the ruler's, at the scale of a person: taking what came through you as stock you own.

`ⲕⲁ̅ⲧ̅ⲁ̅ ⲓ̈ⲱϩ̅ⲁ̅ⲛ̅ⲛⲏ̅ⲛ̅ — ⲁ̅ⲡ̅ⲟⲕⲣ̅ⲩ̅ⲫⲟ̅ⲛ̅`

---

## What the story is

Told through once, it is not a myth about escaping matter. The third folio refuses that frame before the plot begins, and the last movement has the light going *into* the body rather than calling anyone out of it.

It is an account of what happens when the second thing moves before the first — wisdom before will, the word before what the word was for — and of what it costs to put the order back. Everything after the turn is repair. The cosmos is repair. The body is repair, being both the place the disorder finally came to rest and the only place it can be addressed, which is why the third descent has to go all the way down.

And the condition it describes is not wickedness. It is stated twice in the same phrase at the two ends of the book: not knowing the place you came from. The man at the temple knows it, and doubts anyway, and has to leave the temple to be shown. The ruler does not know it, and makes a world out of what he was given while insisting nothing was given. Between those two the whole thing runs.

## Where this telling goes beyond the text

Named, so the reading can be checked.

1. **The frame echo** (`ⲡⲙⲁ ⲉⲧⲁϥⲉⲓ` at fol. 1:11 and fol. 11:22). The lexical parallel is in the manuscript. That the composer *intended* it as an inclusio is my inference, and folio 1 is heavily restored — though the relevant phrase is among the legible parts.
2. **Reading John's departure from the temple as a state rather than a route.** The text gives the movement; the interpretation of it is mine. The words for the destination are restored and the "mountain" familiar from English is not in this manuscript.
3. **The three questions as the book's structural spine.** The questions are in the text (fol. 2:16–18) and the material does fall into that order. That the author organised by them is a reading.
4. **Envy / skin / the powers' envy as one movement.** The three data are in the text and the word is the same in all three. Treating them as a single deliberate construction is my reading.
5. **"Nothing is corrected at a degree above where it landed."** Not stated in the tractate. It is my formulation of why the third descent reaches the body, and it should be marked as such.
6. **The curse on selling read as the ruler's error at personal scale.** The curse is in the text; the parallel is mine.

Six anomalies that complicate this telling — including the mixed register of the Pentad, the two differing accounts of Sophia's restoration, and the fact that this descent is *not commissioned*, unlike the Manichaean pattern — are recorded in §23 of [findings/II_1_apocryphon_john_the_correspondential_teaching.md](findings/II_1_apocryphon_john_the_correspondential_teaching.md) and are not smoothed over here.
